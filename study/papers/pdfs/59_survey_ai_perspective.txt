Abstract
Acoustic Side-Channel Attacks (ASCAs) exploit the sound produced by keyboards and
other devices to infer sensitive information without breaching software or network defenses. Recent advances in deep learning, large language models, and signal processing
have greatly expanded the feasibility and accuracy of these attacks. To clarify the evolving
threat landscape, this survey systematically reviews ASCA research published between
January 2020 and February 2025. We categorize modern ASCA methods into three levels
of text reconstruction—individual keystrokes, short text (words/phrases), and long-text
regeneration— and analyze the signal processing, machine learning, and language-model
decoding techniques that enable them. We also evaluate how environmental factors such as
microphone placement, ambient noise, and keyboard design influence attack performance,
and we examine the challenges of generalizing laboratory-trained models to real-world
settings. This survey makes three primary contributions: (1) it provides the first structured
taxonomy of ASCAs based on text generation granularity and decoding methodology;
(2) it synthesizes cross-study evidence on environmental and hardware factors that fundamentally shape ASCA performance; and (3) it consolidates emerging countermeasures,
including Generative Adversarial Network-based noise masking, cryptographic defenses,
and environmental mitigation, while identifying open research gaps and future threats
posed by voice-enabled IoT and prospective quantum side-channels. Together, these insights underscore the need for interdisciplinary, multi-layered defenses against rapidly
advancing ASCA techniques.
Keywords: acoustic side-channel attacks; keyboard snooping; keystroke analysis;
signal processing

1.1. Problem Statement
In the last decade, significant advancements in deep learning and generative artificial intelligence have emerged, presenting opportunities to develop new attack vectors
in the field of ASCAs and, therefore, new challenges to thwart those attack vectors [7].

of the acoustic side-channel attack field and suggest that a new systematic literature review
is warranted to capture and assess the implications of these technological advancements

2. Background and Concepts
2.1. Overview of Side-Channel Attacks
Side-channel attacks (SCAs) exploit unintended information leakage from physical
systems rather than targeting cryptographic algorithms directly. These attacks capitalize on
the physical properties of computing devices to infer data [13]. SCAs can be categorized
based on the type of emanation they leverage:

•
•
•
•

Electromagnetic SCAs—Utilize electromagnetic emissions from electronic circuits to
extract encryption keys or reconstruct processed data.
Power SCAs—Analyze variations in power consumption to deduce secret operations,
often used in cryptographic attacks (e.g., differential power analysis).
Timing SCAs—Measure execution time variations in software or hardware to infer
cryptographic operations.
Acoustic SCAs—Exploit sound emissions from keyboards, processors, or other components to reconstruct information such as typed keystrokes or encryption processes.

Acoustic side-channel attacks (ASCAs), a subset of SCAs, leverage audio signals to
analyze system behavior, particularly keystroke sounds for text reconstruction, making
them highly relevant in remote work environments where traditional security measures
may not account for such threats [14].
Historical Context
The field of SCAs has evolved over decades, with several groundbreaking discoveries
laying the foundation for acoustic-based attacks.
Power and Timing Attacks on Cryptographic Systems (1990s–2000s)
In 1996, Paul C. Kocher introduced the concept of timing attacks, demonstrating that
by carefully measuring the time required to perform private key operations, attackers could
potentially recover secret keys from cryptographic algorithms such as Diffie-Hellman, RSA,
and DSS. This revelation highlighted that execution time variations could be exploited to
deduce sensitive information [15].
Building upon this, in 1999, Kocher, along with Joshua Jaffe and Benjamin Jun, presented Differential Power Analysis (DPA). They illustrated that by analyzing power con-

sumption patterns during cryptographic operations, attackers could extract secret keys,
thereby demonstrating that power fluctuations could leak cryptographic information. This
work significantly influenced subsequent research on non-intrusive SCAs [16].
Early Acoustic Side-Channel Attacks (2004–2010)
In 2004, Dmitri Asonov and Rakesh Agrawal conducted a study revealing that different keys on a keyboard produce distinctive acoustic signatures. By analyzing these
sound patterns, they demonstrated the feasibility of keystroke recognition through acoustic
emissions, introducing a novel attack vector in the realm of SCAs [17].
The same year, researchers further discovered that acoustic emissions from computer
CPUs during cryptographic operations could be exploited. By listening to the sounds
produced by a computer’s processor, they were able to infer RSA secret keys, underscoring
the potential of acoustic side-channel attacks to compromise cryptographic security [18].
Advancements in Machine Learning for SCAs (2010s–Present)
The 2010s witnessed significant advancements in machine learning, which researchers
leveraged to enhance the effectiveness of SCAs. In 2018, Martin et al. introduced a deep
learning-based approach to keystroke inference attacks, achieving notable accuracy in
classifying keystrokes based on acoustic data. Their study demonstrated the practicality
of using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to process spectrogram representations
of keystroke sounds, thereby making acoustic side-channel attacks more feasible in realworld scenarios [19].
Building upon this foundation, in 2023, Harrison et al. presented a practical implementation of a state-of-the-art deep learning model to classify laptop keystrokes using a
smartphone’s integrated microphone. Their classifier achieved an accuracy of 95% when
trained on keystrokes recorded by a nearby phone and 93% accuracy with recordings from
video-conferencing software like Zoom. This research highlighted the increased accessibility and effectiveness of such attacks, emphasizing the evolving threat landscape posed
by SCAs [7].
These historical developments illustrate how SCAs transitioned from theoretical vulnerabilities to real-world threats, ultimately leading to the emergence of acoustic-based
keystroke inference as a serious cybersecurity concern.
2.2. Acoustic Side-Channels
2.2.1. Basic Principles of Acoustic Side-Channels
Acoustic side-channel attacks (ASCAs) exploit sound waves produced by various
electronic devices to extract sensitive information. These attacks leverage the principle
that physical operations generate unique acoustic signatures, which can be captured,
analyzed, and mapped to specific actions or data. Figure 2 shows an example of how this
is accomplished.
One prominent method is keystroke analysis, where different keyboard keys produce
distinct acoustic signatures due to variations in key size, switch mechanisms, and impact
sounds. By capturing and classifying these sounds, an attacker can reconstruct typed text.
For instance, a study demonstrated that machine learning algorithms could be trained to
identify keystrokes based on their acoustic emissions [20].
Another technique involves microphone-based eavesdropping. Modern microphones,
including those in smartphones, laptops, and smart speakers, can detect subtle acoustic
variations even from a distance. AI-driven filtering techniques enhance recognition accuracy. Research has shown that deep learning models can classify laptop keystrokes with
high accuracy using a smartphone’s integrated microphone [7]. Ref. [18] was also able to

Sound propagation and reflection also play a role in ASCAs. Acoustic waves travel
through air and surfaces, sometimes revealing secondary information about hardware
operations, such as CPU processing sounds or printer movements. An example is the use
of active acoustic side-channel attacks, where speakers emit inaudible acoustic signals, and
the echoes are recorded via microphones to infer information about user interactions [21].
Recent advancements in machine learning and signal processing have drastically
improved the ability to classify sound patterns, making ASCAs more precise and scalable
than traditional statistical methods. Studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of deep
learning models in accurately interpreting acoustic data for side-channel attacks [7].
These developments underscore the evolving nature of ASCAs and the importance of
understanding their mechanisms to develop effective countermeasures.
2.2.2. Typical Hardware Exploitations
Acoustic side-channel attacks can target a wide range of hardware devices that emit
characteristic sounds during their operations. Keyboards, both mechanical and membrane
types, generate distinct keystroke sounds due to their unique construction and switch mechanisms. These sounds can be captured and analyzed to reconstruct typed text. Similarly,
smartphones and smart assistants, equipped with built-in microphones, can be exploited
to eavesdrop on keystrokes and device activities. For instance, the study “Hearing your
touch: A new acoustic side channel on smartphones” demonstrated that the microphones
in smartphones and tablets can capture the sound waves generated by taps on the screen,
allowing attackers to recover what users type on virtual keyboards [22].
Printers and scanners are other targets for ASCAs. The mechanical movements involved in printing or scanning produce unique acoustic signatures, which, if captured,
could potentially reveal the contents of printed text or scanned documents [23]. Even hard
drives and CPUs can emit subtle sound emissions during cryptographic operations, such
as minor variations in CPU cooling fan speed or hard drive actuator movement, which
could leak sensitive information [18].
Additionally, wearable devices, such as smartwatches and fitness trackers, may inadvertently capture acoustic signals from their surroundings, potentially aiding side-channel
attacks by providing data that complements other captured signals [21]. Furthermore,
software-based vulnerabilities arise when applications unintentionally allow unauthorized

microphone access, which amplifies the risks of ASCAs in environments such as remote
work, corporate offices, and secure facilities [8].
2.2.3. Typical Software Exploitations
Acoustic side-channel attacks can expose various cryptographic and software vulnerabilities by exploiting unintended sound emissions from devices. For instance, during
cryptographic operations, the processing noise generated by CPUs can be analyzed to infer
private keys. A notable example is the extraction of 4096-bit RSA decryption keys from
laptops by capturing the acoustic emissions produced during decryption [18].
Attackers can also perform audio-based keylogging by analyzing the acoustic properties of keystrokes to infer typed passwords, PINs, and passphrases. Research has demonstrated that different keys produce distinct sounds, allowing for the reconstruction of
typed information [17].
Furthermore, ASCAs can be combined with adversarial AI techniques to manipulate
or replay audio, potentially bypassing voice-based authentication systems. This approach
highlights the evolving sophistication of attacks targeting biometric security measures [24].
Mobile devices are also susceptible, as smartphone microphones can capture acoustic
variations caused by screen taps, unlocking gestures, and notification sounds, potentially
revealing user activity. The “SonarSnoop” attack exemplifies this, where speakers emit
inaudible acoustic signals, and the echoes are recorded via microphones to infer user
interactions, such as unlock patterns [25].
These vulnerabilities underscore the growing threat posed by ASCAs, especially as
advancements in AI and deep learning enhance attack efficiency. It is crucial to explore
countermeasures and mitigation strategies to address these evolving risks.
2.3. Related Work
The most recent systematic literature review on acoustic side-channel attacks is titled “A Survey on Acoustic Side Channel Attacks on Keyboards”, authored by Alireza
Taheritajar, Zahra Mahmoudpour Harris, and Reza Rahaeimehr. This survey, published on
20 September 2023, provides a comprehensive examination of the susceptibility of mechanical keyboards to acoustic side-channel attacks. It delves into the feasibility of such attacks,
explores various threat models, and discusses methodologies employed to execute and enhance these attacks. The paper emphasizes the increasing vulnerability of keyboard-based
input systems, especially with advancements in microphone technology [22].
Additionally, another pertinent work is “SoK: Acoustic Side Channels” authored by
Ping Wang, Shishir Nagaraja, Aurélien Bourquard, Haichang Gao, and Jeff Yan, published
on 6 August 2023. This study offers a state-of-the-art analysis of acoustic side channels,
encompassing significant academic research in the area, discussing security implications,
countermeasures, and identifying avenues for future research. Notably, it attempts to bridge
the fields of side channels and inverse problems, highlighting their deep connections [23].

3. Systematic Review Methodology
Conducting a systematic literature review is a critical step in understanding the current
state of research on acoustic side-channel attacks. Unlike traditional narrative reviews,
which provide a broad discussion of a topic, an SLR follows a structured, transparent, and
replicable methodology to ensure a comprehensive and unbiased assessment of existing
literature. By systematically identifying, selecting, and synthesizing relevant studies, an
SLR helps map out key findings, highlight research gaps, and establish a solid foundation
for future work.

Ensuring rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria allows the study to provide reliable
insights for both academic researchers and industry practitioners seeking to mitigate ASCArelated security risks or how to create new versions of the attack for research purposes.
When executed properly, a SLR minimizes bias in the search, identification, and analysis
stages, leading to findings that can inform cybersecurity strategies and drive innovation in
defense mechanisms against ASCAs.
This systematic literature review follows the PSALSAR (Protocol, Search, Appraisal,
Synthesis, Analysis, and Reporting) framework to provide a structured and transparent
methodology for evaluating acoustic side-channel attacks. The PSALSAR framework
ensures that each stage of the review is conducted rigorously and systematically. This
approach enhances the reproducibility and reliability of the findings, making them valuable
for both academic research and practical security applications.
This SLR applies the PSALSAR methodology to provide a comprehensive examination
of ASCAs, including their effectiveness at different text granularity levels, the primary
machine learning techniques used in their execution, environmental factors influencing
their success, and existing mitigation strategies. The structured nature of PSALSAR ensures
that the review captures key advancements while identifying trends and gaps in the
literature, offering actionable insights for researchers and cybersecurity practitioners alike.

Screening Process
To ensure a rigorous and comprehensive selection of studies, we employed a multistage screening process designed to progressively refine the pool of relevant literature while
maintaining specificity. This process followed four main phases: Identification, Screening,
Eligibility, and Final Selection.
In the Identification phase, broad keyword searches were conducted to capture the
full lexical and conceptual diversity of research addressing ASCAs within cybersecurity.
Because the terminology used to describe ASCAs has evolved inconsistently across disciplines, spanning computer security, signal processing, and human–computer interaction, it
was necessary to include multiple synonymous or overlapping expressions. The core search
terms (“keystroke inference,” “acoustic cryptanalysis,” “keyboard acoustics,” and “typed
input”) were chosen to encompass both the attack mechanism (e.g., inferring keystrokes
or extracting sensitive data from audio emissions) and the signal domain (e.g., acoustic or
audio-based analysis). Complementary terms such as “signals intelligence” and “acoustic
analysis” were incorporated to account for literature originating from adjacent domains,
including information assurance, hardware security, and surveillance studies, where similar
techniques may be discussed under different nomenclature. Applying these keywords
across titles, abstracts, keywords, and full texts ensured comprehensive retrieval of relevant
works regardless of the terminology adopted by individual authors or publication venues.

Additionally, priority is given to peer-reviewed articles, conference proceedings, and
high-impact journals from reputable academic databases, including IEEE Xplore, ACM
Digital Library, Scopus, Springer, and arXiv. This ensures that the included studies meet
rigorous academic standards. Research published in top-tier cybersecurity conferences or

3.3.2. Exclusion Criteria
Duplicate studies identified across multiple databases were removed, with preference
given to the most complete, peer-reviewed, and recently updated version of each work.
Research that was only tangentially related to ASCAs was excluded, including studies
on side-channel attacks that did not involve acoustic methods. Terms such as “power
analysis” and “electromagnetic emissions” were explicitly excluded, as these attacks do
not utilize acoustic signals and therefore fall outside the scope of this review. Incomplete
or insufficiently detailed studies, such as abstracts without full-text availability, preprints
lacking clear methodology sections, or papers that provided no empirical or conceptual
contribution to acoustic-based inference, were also filtered out during screening. These exclusion measures ensured that only high-quality, methodologically relevant ASCA research
was retained for full analysis.

4.2. Technological and Methodical Trends
The studies analyzed in this systematic literature review reveal consistent patterns
in both hardware and software exploited in ASCAs. In terms of hardware, laptops and
desktops are among the most commonly targeted devices. Several studies focus on capturing keystroke acoustic signatures from laptop keyboards using smartphone microphones
or online conferencing software. For example, A Practical Deep Learning-Based Acoustic
Side Channel Attack on Keyboards demonstrates how a smartphone microphone can capture and classify keystrokes with 95% accuracy [7], while A New Deep Learning Pipeline
for Acoustic Attack on Keyboards uses Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) to extract keystroke data with high precision [19]. Similarly, Acoustic Side Channel Attack
for Keystroke Splitting in the Wild emphasizes the importance of smart segmentation
techniques to classify keystrokes effectively [26].
Beyond traditional computing devices, mobile and IoT devices present dual
vulnerabilities—both as attack vectors and targets. Smartphones, for instance, can be
exploited for microphone-based eavesdropping, where inaudible ultrasound signals capture PIN entries with a 75% success rate [27]. Additionally, Keyboard Acoustic Side Channel
Attacks on Android Phones in the AI Era of Digital Banking highlights how side-channel
attacks can extract PINs and passwords from Android devices [28]. Meanwhile, IoT devices
such as robot vacuum cleaners have been repurposed for eavesdropping, as demonstrated
in LidarPhone Acoustic Eavesdropping Using a Lidar Sensor, where lidar reflections from
household objects were used to reconstruct speech with up to 91% accuracy [29]. This
underscores how seemingly benign smart devices can become powerful surveillance tools.
Industrial and cyber-physical systems (CPSs) are also vulnerable to ASCAs, particularly in additive manufacturing (AM) environments. Security Threats in AM discusses
how acoustic emissions from 3D printers and CNC machines can reveal proprietary design
details, leading to risks such as design theft, sabotage, and illegal part manufacturing [30].
These findings highlight the growing risk of intellectual property theft and industrial
espionage through non-traditional attack vectors.
From a software perspective, several studies expose vulnerabilities in operating systems and application security. Many attacks exploit weak sensor permission policies in
mobile OS environments, allowing unauthorized apps to access microphones, gyroscopes,
or lidar sensors for eavesdropping. Keyboard Acoustic Side Channel Attacks on Android

4.3. Attack Outcomes and Severity
ASCAs can be classified based on their impact on security objectives. For instance,
attacks that reconstruct keystroke signals or PIN entries primarily compromise confidentiality and privacy by exposing sensitive data. Notable case studies include the PinDrop
attack, which demonstrated that acoustic emanations from ATM PIN pads can be exploited
to recover up to 96% of 4-digit and 94% of 5-digit PINs at close range [32], and the UltraPIN
attack that infers PIN entries from inaudible ultrasound signals on smartphones [27]. In
contrast, attacks targeting the operational data of systems, such as those exploiting acoustic
emissions from 3D printers, pose a threat to integrity by potentially enabling design theft
or unauthorized modifications of product specifications. For example, Security Threats
in AM reports cases of design theft via the acoustic emanations of a 3D printer and sabotage of a quadcopter by targeting its 3D-printed propeller, illustrating vulnerabilities in
additive manufacturing systems, although real-world exploitation in this area remains
largely experimental [30].
Additionally, while availability is less commonly the direct target of ASCAs, disruptions to hardware behavior (for example, by sabotaging a 3D-printed propeller) illustrate
how such attacks can indirectly affect system availability. Previous research on additive
manufacturing security has demonstrated how attackers can introduce selective defects in
design files, compromising the structural integrity of printed components, including critical
aerospace and automotive parts [30]. These case studies underscore the multifaceted risks
of ASCAs and highlight the urgent need for comprehensive security measures that address
confidentiality, integrity, availability, and privacy simultaneously.

4.4. Acoustic Channels vs. Other Side Channels
Acoustic side-channel attacks vary widely in their approaches, and comparing their
effectiveness, feasibility, and stealth reveals distinct trade-offs that depend on the target environment and the attacker’s operational constraints. Some methods, such as the
PinDrop attack, focus on high accuracy in PIN reconstruction, whereas others, like UltraPIN, prioritize stealth by exploiting inaudible ultrasound signals. Deep learning-based
keystroke recognition pipelines further push the boundaries of accuracy but at the expense
of increased complexity and dependence on extensive training data.
The PinDrop attack exemplifies a highly effective method when conducted at close
range. In controlled experiments, PinDrop was shown to recover up to 96% of 4-digit PINs
and 94% of 5-digit PINs when the recording device was positioned 0.3 m from the target.
However, its effectiveness decreases significantly with distance—recovering only 57% of
4-digit and 39% of 5-digit PINs at 2 m [32]. This method’s feasibility is largely contingent on
the attacker being in close proximity and operating in a relatively noise-free environment,
which, while achievable in some settings, limits its applicability in more variable realworld scenarios.
In contrast, the UltraPIN attack leverages inaudible ultrasound signals to capture
keystroke data from a distance ranging between 50 and 100 cm [27]. Although UltraPIN
achieves a somewhat lower success rate of around 75% in recovering PIN entries, it offers a
significant advantage in terms of stealth. The inaudibility of ultrasound ensures that the
attack remains covert, minimizing the chance of detection by the victim. This trade-off
between lower effectiveness and higher stealth can be particularly attractive in scenarios
where remaining undetected is critical.
Deep learning-based approaches have significantly advanced the accuracy of acoustic
side-channel attacks, particularly with the adoption of Temporal Convolutional Networks
(TCNs). In [31], TCNs were utilized to model long-range dependencies in keystroke
acoustic emissions, achieving a peak accuracy of 98.3% under controlled conditions. This
study highlights the capability of TCNs in keystroke recognition but also notes challenges
related to dataset quality, environmental noise, and real-world variability. Similarly, ref. [7]
demonstrated the effectiveness of deep learning for keystroke classification, reaching
95% accuracy when using recordings from nearby smartphone microphones and 93%
accuracy with Zoom-based recordings. This underscores the feasibility of deep learningdriven ASCAs even in remote attack scenarios. Additionally, ref. [26] applied TCNs
for keystroke classification and segmentation, showcasing high precision in recognizing
individual keystrokes despite environmental noise. These studies collectively demonstrate
the potential of TCN-based ASCAs while also highlighting their reliance on well-labeled
datasets and the need for robust preprocessing techniques to mitigate real-world challenges.
Overall, while PinDrop excels in scenarios requiring high accuracy at very close
distances, UltraPIN provides enhanced stealth through the use of inaudible signals, and
deep learning pipelines offer superior accuracy under ideal conditions, each method comes
with its own set of limitations. The choice of ASCA method ultimately depends on the
specific priorities of the attacker—whether effectiveness, feasibility, or stealth is most
critical in the given context. Future research must address these trade-offs by developing
methods that can generalize better to real-world conditions and by proposing robust
countermeasures to mitigate these emerging threats.

the practical deployment of such modifications remains challenging, especially in public

easily controlled.

5. Discussion
5.1. Research Question #1
How effective are ASCAs in reconstructing typed inputs at different levels of text
granularity (individual letters, short text, and large text)?
Answer: Our review of the 19 papers reveals that ASCAs are highly effective in
reconstructing typed inputs at the level of individual letters under controlled conditions.
Several studies employing deep learning methods, particularly Temporal Convolutional
Networks (TCNs), have reported peak accuracies up to 98.3% in extracting keystroke
events from laptop keyboards [31]. This high level of performance demonstrates that when
environmental conditions are favorable and signal quality is high, ASCAs can reliably
reconstruct the individual components of a typed input.
When it comes to short text—such as words, phrases, or PIN entries—the literature
shows promising results as well. Notable case studies, including those on the [27,32] attacks,
have achieved recovery rates of up to 96% for 4-digit PINs and 94% for 5-digit PINs at close
range. These studies highlight that even with a slight increase in input complexity, acoustic
methods can still yield highly accurate reconstructions, albeit with some degradation in
performance as the distance from the source increases or environmental noise rises.
However, our synthesis indicates a significant research gap regarding the reconstruction of large text, such as full sentences or paragraphs. None of the reviewed papers specifically address the challenges associated with scaling keystroke reconstruction to longer,
more complex texts. While studies such as [7,31] demonstrate high accuracy in individual
keystroke classification, and research on [27,32] shows the feasibility of reconstructing short
text sequences like PINs, their application to continuous text remains unexamined. This
suggests that while current ASCA techniques are well-developed for extracting individual
characters and short sequences, their extension to large text reconstruction remains an
underexplored area that warrants further investigation.
The effectiveness of ASCAs is closely tied to the environmental conditions under
which the attacks are executed. Many studies, such as [7,27] report high accuracy in lab
settings with controlled noise and optimal sensor placement, but their performance tends to
degrade in real-world conditions where factors like ambient noise, varied keyboard designs,
and user typing behaviors introduce additional challenges. This variability underscores the
need for more robust, adaptable techniques that can maintain high performance outside
the laboratory.
In summary, the current body of research demonstrates that ASCAs are highly effective
for reconstructing individual letters and short texts through advanced deep learning and
statistical machine learning methods. Nonetheless, the reconstruction of large text remains
an open challenge, and the variability in environmental conditions further complicates the
generalizability of these results. Future work should address these gaps by exploring new
techniques and developing more resilient models to extend the applicability of ASCAs to
more complex text reconstruction tasks.

What are the primary analysis techniques (e.g., statistical machine learning, deep

each analysis technique?
Answer: The literature reveals that the primary analysis techniques for ASCAs fall
into two main categories: deep learning-based methods and statistical ML approaches.
Deep learning techniques, particularly those employing TCNs, have demonstrated high
accuracy in keystroke inference tasks. For instance, Harrison et al. [7] and Spata et al. [31]
report peak accuracies of up to 98.3% in controlled experiments by leveraging TCNs to
model complex temporal dependencies in keystroke acoustics. These deep learning models
can automatically extract high-level features from raw signals, reducing reliance on manual
feature engineering.
Conversely, statistical ML methods—such as those employed in PinDrop [32], UltraPIN [27], and LidarPhone [29]—utilize classifiers like logistic regression, support vector
machines (SVMs), and other conventional learning algorithms. These approaches are
particularly effective in PIN inference tasks, achieving success rates as high as 96% for
4-digit PINs at close range. However, statistical methods tend to struggle with highly
variable or noisy environments due to their limited ability to generalize across different
acoustic conditions.
Deep learning models outperform statistical ML techniques in terms of accuracy
and adaptability, especially when trained on large, high-quality datasets. However, they
come with significant computational costs and require substantial labeled training data,
making real-world deployment challenging. In contrast, statistical ML approaches are
computationally lighter and can be implemented with less training data, but they require
careful feature selection and may lack the flexibility needed for diverse scenarios.
In summary, deep learning–based methods have achieved the highest success rates
in controlled conditions, while statistical ML techniques remain competitive for simpler
tasks such as PIN inference. Nevertheless, challenges related to scalability, environmental
adaptability, and data availability persist, underscoring the need for continued research
into more efficient and resilient ASCA techniques.

the misclassification of keystrokes. Similarly, variability in keyboard hardware, including
differences in key noise levels and mechanical design, introduces additional complexity
that many studies note as a challenge [34].

5.4. Research Question #4
What active (encryption, noise injection, adversarial training) and passive (hardware
shielding, microphone design) countermeasures exist for ASCAs, and how effective are
they against ML-driven attacks?
Answer: The reviewed literature proposes a range of countermeasures and mitigation strategies against ASCAs, which can be broadly classified into active and passive
approaches. Active countermeasures typically involve software-based defenses that dynamically obscure or alter the acoustic signatures. For example, ref. [33] introduces a
method using generative adversarial networks (GANs) to generate false keystroke sounds.
This active approach helps mask the real acoustic signals, reducing the effectiveness of
attackers’ classification models without necessitating hardware modifications.
Passive countermeasures focus on reducing the physical leakage of acoustic signals.
Hardware-based solutions may include sound-dampening materials, redesigned keyboards,
or improved microphone shielding, though these are less commonly proposed in the literature due to cost and retrofitting challenges [11,33]. Cryptographic approaches, such as
randomized keypads or one-time passwords, do not prevent signal leakage per se but ensure
that even if keystroke data are intercepted, they cannot be easily exploited. Environmental
countermeasures, like generating ambient noise or strategically positioning devices, also play
a supplementary role in reducing the effective SNR available to an attacker [28,35].
Each category of countermeasure has its strengths and limitations. Active, softwarebased methods are more flexible and can be deployed via updates, but they must strike a
balance between security and usability to avoid degrading the user experience [33]. Passive
methods, while offering robust protection by minimizing physical leakage, are challenging
to implement on legacy systems and may impact device performance or ergonomics [11,28].
The literature suggests that an effective defense against ASCAs will likely require a multilayered strategy that combines these approaches, though most current solutions have only
been validated under controlled laboratory conditions [7,31].
In summary, while various countermeasures show promise in mitigating ASCAs, no
single approach is sufficient. The current state-of-the-art primarily features active, softwarebased defenses, yet these methods must be enhanced to address the challenges posed by
real-world variability and sophisticated machine learning–driven attacks.

Despite these advances, several gaps remain. Notably, the literature reveals a lack
of research on reconstructing large text sequences (sentences or paragraphs) from acoustic data, as all the reviewed studies predominantly focus on individual letters or short
text [7,19,31]. Moreover, alternative analysis techniques such as geometric-based or ciphertext analysis remain underexplored, with most studies relying on deep learning or traditional statistical methods [11,12,34]. This gap indicates potential avenues for future research
to explore more diverse analytical frameworks.
Emerging domains such as voice-controlled IoT devices and AR/VR systems, as highlighted by the GAZEploit study [36], are also underrepresented in the current body of
literature. As these technologies become more widespread, their unique acoustic vulnerabilities will likely present new challenges that are not addressed by existing methods.
Furthermore, the rapid pace of AI advancements, especially in self-supervised learning
and transformer-based architectures, offers promising new approaches that have yet to be
fully integrated into ASCA research [8,9]. Expanding research in these areas will be highly
useful to understand and mitigate future ASCAs.
The current mitigation strategies, predominantly software-based, also highlight a
trend toward using deep learning models for both attack and defense. However, their
practical deployment is hindered by the reliance on large, high-quality datasets and the
difficulty of replicating controlled experimental conditions in real-world environments.
This gap between laboratory results and real-world performance calls for further research
into robust, adaptable countermeasures.
In summary, while the literature from 2020 onward demonstrates significant progress
in the development of sophisticated ASCA techniques, key gaps remain—particularly
in large text reconstruction, alternative analysis methods, and the exploration of emerging application domains. Future research should focus on bridging these gaps by incorporating novel AI architectures and validating proposed methods under diverse, realworld conditions. These trends and gaps have important implications for academic research, industry practices, and regulatory frameworks, underscoring the need for integrated, multidisciplinary approaches to improve security in an increasingly interconnected
digital landscape.
5.6. Emerging Avenues in ASCA Research
Our review indicates that several promising areas in acoustic side-channel attack research remain underexplored. Although many studies concentrate on traditional keystrokebased attacks on laptops and mobile devices, as evidenced by the high accuracies reported
in papers like [7], few have ventured into the domain of voice-controlled IoT devices.
As smart speakers and voice assistants proliferate, their inherent acoustic vulnerabilities
represent a fertile ground for future research. Similarly, while our review covers various
traditional and deep learning-based approaches, the potential side-channels arising from
emerging quantum computing technologies such as quantum noise or qubit measurement
leakage have not been adequately investigated. These avenues suggest that as new technologies mature, novel ASCA vulnerabilities may emerge, demanding proactive research
to understand and mitigate them.
5.7. Expanding the Attack Surface Beyond Traditional Devices
Another underexplored area is the use of augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality
(VR) devices. For instance, the GAZEploit study [36] demonstrates that eye-tracking data

in VR/MR environments can be exploited for keystroke inference, indicating a shift in
the types of devices vulnerable to side-channel attacks. Despite this intriguing result, the
broader implications for AR/VR systems have yet to be fully examined, particularly as
these devices become more integrated into everyday activities. Future research should
focus on identifying specific attack vectors within these environments and developing
corresponding countermeasures [8,9,28].
5.8. Integrating Cross-Modal and Industrial Perspectives
While several studies in our review address attacks on additive manufacturing (AM)
systems, such as those highlighted in [30], the intersection of multiple side-channel modalities (e.g., acoustic, electromagnetic, and thermal) in industrial and cyber-physical systems
remains largely uncharted. The complexity of modern manufacturing, where devices often
operate in noisy and variable conditions, poses unique challenges that current research
has not fully addressed. Exploring cross-modal signal processing techniques combined
with advanced AI could unlock new insights into both attack methodologies and robust
mitigation strategies in these environments [9,10,13,37].
5.9. Evaluating Current Mitigation Strategies in Real-World Settings
Regarding countermeasures, the majority of our reviewed papers propose softwarebased solutions such as deep learning techniques—TCNs and GAN-based defenses, as
seen in [7,33]. Although these methods have shown impressive accuracy in controlled
experiments, their feasibility in real-world environments remains uncertain. Factors such as
ambient noise, diverse user behavior, and hardware variability often hinder these defenses
when deployed outside laboratory settings [11,20,31]. This gap suggests a critical need
for extensive field evaluations and the development of hybrid solutions that integrate
hardware, software, and cryptographic methods [15,26,30].
5.10. Synthesis and Future Directions
In summary, while current mitigation strategies demonstrate significant promise under
ideal conditions, they often fall short in addressing the complexities of real-world scenarios.
The emerging vulnerabilities in voice-controlled IoT devices [28], AR/VR systems [31], and
quantum computing platforms [9] highlight the necessity for proactive and adaptive research in ASCA. Future work should prioritize validating these countermeasures in diverse
operational settings and exploring multi-layered defense strategies that can effectively
protect against evolving acoustic side-channel threats. By integrating insights from both
traditional and emerging domains—as well as leveraging cross-modal defenses—we can
work toward more resilient and comprehensive security solutions.
Following the completion of this review, a new study titled “Making Acoustic SideChannel Attacks on Noisy Keyboards Viable with LLM-Assisted Spectrograms’ Typo
Correction” [38] introduced an innovative pipeline that integrates large language models
into the ASCA decoding process to correct textual errors from noisy keyboard recordings.
The work employs a hybrid architecture combining mel-spectrogram feature extraction, a
CoAtNet-based keystroke classifier, and LLM-driven post-processing to refine predicted
text. Specifically, models such as Llama-3 and GPT-4o were fine-tuned using low-rank
adaptation (QLoRA) to correct typographical and semantic inconsistencies in keystrokederived text. While this demonstrates a promising direction for leveraging LLMs as
lightweight error-correction agents within ASCA pipelines, the study relies on the Harrison
et al. dataset [7], which this review previously identified as having limitations due to its
small participant size and lack of full words, phrases, or sentences.
Consequently, while the new research advances the integration of generative AI into
ASCA post-processing, its dependency on constrained data reinforces this review’s earlier

observation that progress in the field is limited by the lack of realistic, publicly available
datasets. The benchmark dataset by Quattrone and Badr (2025) [39] better reflects realistic typing conditions by incorporating paragraph-level audio and keylogger recordings.
Leveraging such datasets in future work will be essential for evaluating LLM-assisted error
correction under realistic operating conditions and ensuring the external validity of these
promising techniques.
5.11. Challenges and Implications
ASCAs present several technical challenges that hinder both their research and realworld deployment. In controlled environments, experiments like those detailed in the deep
learning-based approaches [7] have achieved impressive accuracies. However, issues such
as reliability and scalability remain significant obstacles. For example, while Temporal
Convolutional Networks (TCNs) have demonstrated peak accuracies of up to 98.3% [31],
these results are often obtained under highly controlled conditions with high-quality
recordings. In real-world scenarios, environmental noise—ranging from ambient sound to
variations in keyboard hardware—can severely degrade signal quality, making accurate
keystroke reconstruction challenging and reducing overall reliability.
Ethical and legal issues also come to the forefront in the context of ASCAs. The ability
to infer sensitive data such as passwords and PINs, as shown in studies like [32], raises
substantial data privacy concerns. The unauthorized interception of keystroke data can lead
to breaches of personal and corporate confidentiality, challenging existing data protection
frameworks. At the same time, research in this field must navigate ethical hacking norms,
ensuring that experimental procedures do not inadvertently violate privacy rights or facilitate
malicious exploitation. The balance between advancing scientific knowledge and protecting
individual privacy is delicate, necessitating clear ethical guidelines and robust oversight.
Standardization and policy considerations further complicate the landscape. Despite
significant advancements in the detection and mitigation of ASCAs, there is a notable lack
of unified standards. For instance, current regulatory frameworks, such as the FIPS 140-3
draft [15], have yet to incorporate acoustic emanations in their security guidelines, leaving
a critical gap in standardization efforts. This absence of standard policies hampers the
adoption of effective countermeasures in commercial products and creates uncertainty
regarding the best practices for defending against ASCAs. Harmonizing technical standards
with policy directives is essential to ensure that mitigation strategies can be deployed
effectively across various industries.
Additionally, the complexity of ASCAs requires an interdisciplinary approach,
merging insights from hardware design, signal processing, and cybersecurity policy.
The reviewed literature suggests that a multi-layered defense strategy—which combines hardware-based dampening, software-based noise injection, and cryptographic
methods—may be the most promising avenue for countering ASCAs [7,11,19]. However,
the lack of field studies and long-term evaluations in real-world conditions underscores
the need for further research. Such studies would not only validate the efficacy of these
countermeasures under diverse environmental conditions but also inform policymakers
about necessary regulatory updates [15,33].
In conclusion, while current research demonstrates that advanced machine learning techniques can achieve high accuracy in keystroke reconstruction under controlled
settings [7,19,31], the transition to real-world deployment is hampered by challenges in
reliability, scalability, and environmental noise [11,33]. Ethical and legal issues, particularly
regarding data privacy and responsible disclosure, further complicate the application of
these technologies [32,40]. The absence of standardized guidelines calls for concerted
efforts among researchers, industry stakeholders, and regulatory bodies to develop inte-

https://doi.org/10.3390/jcp6010006

grated, adaptable frameworks that address the technical, ethical, and policy dimensions
of ASCAs [13,15,30]. Addressing these multifaceted challenges is crucial for developing
robust, comprehensive security solutions that can effectively mitigate the risks posed by
acoustic side-channel attacks.
The findings from these studies carry important implications for academia, industry, and regulatory bodies. For the research community, the high accuracies achieved by
deep learning pipelines and specialized signal processing methods highlight the technical
feasibility of ASCAs, yet also expose critical gaps such as scalability under diverse environmental conditions and generalization to natural, unconstrained inputs that require further
investigation. Papers like A New Deep Learning Pipeline for Acoustic Attack on Keyboards [19] and Acoustic Side Channel Attack on Keyboards Based on Typing Patterns [35]
serve as both benchmarks and calls to action for interdisciplinary work combining signal
processing, machine learning, and hardware design. The availability of comprehensive
datasets such as the MKA Datasets [4] provides a valuable resource for further studies and
benchmarking, while the documented limitations urge the development of standardized
protocols for both attack simulation and mitigation.
For industry and regulators, the demonstrated vulnerabilities, whether in traditional
computing devices, mobile phones, or even emerging IoT platforms as shown by UltraPIN [27], LidarPhone [29], and Keyboard Acoustic Side Channel Attacks on Android
Phones in the AI Era of Digital Banking [28], underscore the urgency for proactive security measures. The potential for acoustic eavesdropping to compromise sensitive data,
including PINs and keystrokes, calls for the integration of robust hardware, software, and
cryptographic defenses into the design of new devices. Additionally, regulatory frameworks such as those under FIPS 140-3 must evolve to consider non-traditional leakage
channels like acoustics [6]. As these studies collectively indicate, a multi-layered, standardized approach to security, encompassing rigorous testing, cross-disciplinary research, and
updated legal and regulatory guidelines, is essential to mitigate the complex risks posed by
ASCAs in an increasingly connected digital environment.

6. Conclusions
This systematic literature review provides a comprehensive and updated analysis of
acoustic side-channel attacks from recent breakthroughs in artificial intelligence and reveals
a broad spectrum of techniques for executing and analyzing sophisticated attacks. Notable
attacks include Your PIN Sounds Good! (PinDrop) and UltraPIN Inferring PIN Entries via
Ultrasound, which demonstrate that acoustic emanations from keyboards or PIN pads can
be exploited with high accuracy to recover 4-digit PINs in close-range scenarios.
In parallel, deep learning-based architectures such as Temporal Convolutional Networks have achieved remarkable accuracies under controlled conditions, underscoring
the technical feasibility of using advanced machine learning to extract keystroke patterns.
Meanwhile, recent attacks that model typing patterns operate in the frequency domain,
exploit inter-keystroke timing, or use geometric/triangulation techniques and illustrate
different trade-offs in accuracy, robustness to noise and room acoustics, data requirements,
and cross-device transferability.
A notable trend emerging from the literature is the diversification of targets and attack
platforms. In addition to traditional laptops and desktops, researchers have demonstrated
attacks on mobile devices and IoT platforms. For example, Keyboard Acoustic Side Channel
Attacks on Android Phones (i.e., Digital Banking and LidarPhone with a Lidar Sensor) have
successfully exploited the ubiquity of smartphones and even repurposed smart devices like
robot vacuum cleaners for eavesdropping. Furthermore, specialized datasets, such as the
Multi-Keyboard Acoustic (MKA) Datasets, have enabled robust training and evaluation of

https://doi.org/10.3390/jcp6010006

acoustic attack models, although challenges remain in capturing real-world variability and
environmental noise.
This review highlights that the field’s progress is increasingly constrained by the
limited availability of large, realistic, and publicly accessible datasets. Many foundational
corpora, including widely reused collections like Harrison et al. [7], lack essential features
such as natural typing behavior, the full range of available keyboard keys, environmental
diversity, and simultaneous keystroke–audio synchronization. As a result, high reported
accuracies often fail to be generalized beyond laboratory settings.
To address these limitations, emerging datasets such as the Synchronized Keystroke and
Acoustic Inference Dataset (SKAID) represent an important step forward [39]. SKAID provides
long-form, user-generated text, synchronized keystroke logs, high-fidelity audio, demographic
metadata, and recordings across multiple environments. These characteristics make it wellsuited for evaluating the robustness of ASCA pipelines, benchmarking deep learning models
under realistic conditions, and enabling research on long-text inference. Incorporating datasets
of this nature into future ASCA research will be essential for improving ecological validity,
advancing model generalization, and supporting reproducible experimentation.
